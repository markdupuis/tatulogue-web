<hr>
<p>"What should I get for my first tattoo?" is the question that stalls more first-timers than the needle ever does. People sit on it for years. They scroll, save, unsave, and wait for some deep meaning to land.</p>
<p>Here's the short answer: it doesn't need one. Get something you like looking at, built bold enough to hold, from an artist who does that style well.</p>
<p>This post is only about the design. If you want the rest of it, the consult, deposits, what shop day looks like and aftercare, that's all in our <a href="/blog/first-tattoo-guide">first tattoo guide</a>. Here we're picking the thing that goes on your skin.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Does your first tattoo need to mean something?</h2>
<p>No. Your first tattoo doesn't always have to have meaning. Plenty of people get a panther, a rose or a dagger because it looks good, and that's a completely legit reason. A design you love the look of will often age better in your head than a heavy symbol you picked to justify it.</p>
<p>The pressure to make it meaningful comes from the internet, not the shop. Walk into any street shop and look at the walls. Swallows, skulls, roses, eagles, snakes. Most of the people wearing those didn't write an essay about them first.</p>
<p>And meaning shifts anyway. A thing that feels huge at 22 can feel small at 35. A tattoo you got because it was a banger stays a banger.</p>
<p>So if the only thing stopping you is that you can't find the "right" meaning, stop looking. Pick what you'd want to see every morning.</p>
<h2>First tattoo ideas that age well: classic and traditional</h2>
<p>Classic designs and traditional flash are some of the safest first tattoo ideas there are. Bold black outlines, solid fills and simple shapes keep reading after years of sun and skin changes. Tiny tight detail is what goes first, so a design built bold has more room to age.</p>
<p>That isn't just shop folklore. Tattooer Adam Villani told <a href="https://www.bustle.com/style/7-tattoos-that-actually-look-better-as-you-age-according-to-tattoo-artists-11941315" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bustle</a> that bold black text and traditional American tattoos still look good even once they fade. In the same piece, tattooer Toby Gehrlich warned that small, tight, intricate details will be lost with time, while larger designs stay readable.</p>
<p>Traditional imagery has also been tattooed for so long that it never really goes out of style. A rose is a rose. A swallow is a swallow. Nobody looks at a clean trad panther in 15 years and wonders what year you got it. If you want the history and the classic motifs, we broke it down in our <a href="/blog/american-traditional-tattoos">American traditional tattoos</a> post.</p>
<p>You don't have to go full trad, either. "Classic" just means a design that was good before you saw it and will still be good after. Bold always holds.</p>
<p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1200" height="1800" style="max-width:min(100%,373px)" src="/images/blog/what-to-get-for-your-first-tattoo-1.jpg" alt="Tattoo artist with a shaved head drawing at a desk, surrounded by framed traditional flash of roses, snakes, panthers, eagles and lady heads on a pink wall"></p>
<h2>Are animals a good first tattoo?</h2>
<p>Animals are one of the most common first picks, and for good reason. A panther, wolf, snake, eagle, bird or your own dog gives an artist a strong shape to work with, and every style has its own take on them. Pick the style first, then the animal.</p>
<p>A trad eagle and a black and grey realism wolf are completely different tattoos with completely different needs. The trad version can live small on a forearm and still read. A realistic animal needs size so the fur, eyes and shading have room. Squeeze realism down to coin size and it turns to mush.</p>
<p>Your own pet is a great first piece. Just know that the more it looks like <em>your</em> specific dog, the closer you are to portrait territory, and that's a different conversation. More on that next.</p>
<h2>Tattoos for loved ones: tributes and portraits</h2>
<p>A tribute to someone you love is one of the strongest first tattoo ideas, because it's one you're unlikely to fall out of love with. But there are two very different routes here. A portrait, and everything that isn't a portrait. They need different artists and different budgets.</p>
<h3>If you want a portrait, pay for it</h3>
<p>If you're getting a portrait, make sure you go to someone reputable and be willing to pay for the expertise. This is the one place where shopping on price will burn you. A face that's slightly off doesn't look like a rough tattoo. It looks like a stranger.</p>
<p>Even shops that do general work say it straight. <a href="https://certifiedtattoo.com/blog/getting-a-portrait-tattoo-heres-what-you-need-to-know" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Certified Tattoo Studios</a> calls portraits very difficult to do well and says to find an artist who specializes in portraiture with real photorealism experience. That same studio blog puts portrait work at roughly $150 to $250 an hour and says most portraits take at least two sessions spaced weeks apart. That's one shop's figure, so treat it as a ballpark, not a quote.</p>
<p>What that means for you: look for an artist whose feed is full of healed portraits, not one portrait buried between roses. Bring sharp, well-lit reference photos. And if the budget isn't there yet, wait and save. A cheap portrait of your grandmother is a hard thing to live with. Our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-vet-a-tattoo-artist">how to vet a tattoo artist</a> walks through what to check before you hand anyone a deposit.</p>
<h3>Tributes that aren't portraits</h3>
<p>You don't need a face to honor someone. Some of the best tribute pieces are quieter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Handwriting.</strong> A signature or a line from a card, in their actual hand.</li>
<li><strong>Dates.</strong> A birthday or a date that matters, in numerals or Roman numerals.</li>
<li><strong>Birth flowers.</strong> Their birth month flower, done as a bold rose or a clean botanical.</li>
<li><strong>Something they loved.</strong> Their dog, their tools, the car they drove, the song they always played.</li>
</ul>
<p>The catch: make sure the design can support itself. Handwriting looks great on paper, but a thin scrawl tattooed tiny can blur into a smudge. Ask your artist to bump the size or weight up so it holds. A birth flower with one hundred fine petals at thumbnail size has the same problem.</p>
<p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1200" height="1800" style="max-width:min(100%,373px)" src="/images/blog/what-to-get-for-your-first-tattoo-2.jpg" alt="Two tattooed forearms held up side by side, covered in bold colour Japanese style work with cherry blossoms, a lotus, clouds and a demon mask"></p>
<h2>What should you not get for your first tattoo?</h2>
<p>Don't get something trendy, and don't get your girlfriend's, boyfriend's or theyfriend's name. Those are the two most reliable ways to end up with a tattoo you're hiding, covering or paying to laser off. Both feel like forever at the time. The feeling usually doesn't last. The ink does.</p>
<h3>Trends</h3>
<p>Trend chasing is how you end up with the infinity sign of your generation. Whatever is blowing up on your feed right now is getting tattooed on thousands of people at the same time, and it'll date you the second the trend moves on.</p>
<p>The bigger problem is that a lot of trendy work is built to photograph, not to age. Micro detail, ultra fine lines, tiny script. It looks crispy in a fresh photo and blurry a few years later, which is exactly what Gehrlich warned about. If you want the long version of what holds and what doesn't, read <a href="/blog/how-tattoos-age">how tattoos age</a> before you commit to something you saw last Tuesday.</p>
<p>Liking a trend isn't a crime. Just ask yourself whether you'd still want it if nobody else had it.</p>
<h3>Your partner's name</h3>
<p>This is the one every tattooer has a story about. You love them now. Great. Get a tattoo that's about you.</p>
<p>Removal shops will tell you who walks through their door. Jason Walker, who owns two tattoo removal businesses in Massachusetts, told the <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2023/09/29/metro/tattoo-remorse-get-rid-it/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Boston Globe</a> that some of his removal work comes down to ex-boyfriends or ex-girlfriends, plus wedding rings tattooed on the ring finger after a divorce. He described a client who wanted an ex-wife removed from his upper back, because the new girlfriend wasn't happy about it.</p>
<p>That's the whole problem in one sentence. Even when the relationship works out, a name is a lot of pressure to put on a person. And when it doesn't, the name becomes the first thing you explain to the next one. <a href="/blog/laser-tattoo-removal-guide">Lasering it off</a> is a whole project, and a <a href="/blog/tattoo-cover-ups">blast over</a> limits what you can get next. Skip it.</p>
<h2>How do you pick a first tattoo from an artist's flash?</h2>
<p>The easiest way to land a good first tattoo is to pick something the artist already drew. Flash is pre-drawn work an artist designed to be tattooed, and many artists also keep a list of wannados, pieces they personally want to tattoo. Pick from either and you get the artist at their best.</p>
<p>Flash is the shop wall classic. The artist already sized it, already knows how it sits, and has usually tattooed it before. You're not asking them to fix your Pinterest screenshot. You're picking something they built to work on skin.</p>
<p>Wannados are the other side of the same idea. A wannado is a design or idea the artist wants to do more of, or wants to experiment with. Some booking platforms now let artists list them for sale too. <a href="https://cocreate.ink/post/how-to-run-a-wanna-do-drop-using-cocreate" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CO:CREATE's guide to Wanna Do drops</a> describes artists setting the design, price and booking window, with clients buying a design and then heading to the artist's scheduling.</p>
<p>Why it's a smart first move: an artist who's excited about the design tends to bring their best hand to it. And you skip a lot of back and forth on the drawing. Find an artist whose style you love, watch their flash and wannado posts, and grab one that speaks to you.</p>
<h2>What size and placement work best for a first tattoo?</h2>
<p>Go a bit bigger than you think and pick a spot that's easy to heal and slow to wear. The flat of the forearm, upper arm, shoulder, back and thigh are common first spots. They give the design room and stay away from high-friction areas like hands and feet.</p>
<p>That list isn't random. In the <a href="https://www.bustle.com/style/7-tattoos-that-actually-look-better-as-you-age-according-to-tattoo-artists-11941315" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bustle piece</a>, Gehrlich named the flat of the forearm, upper arms, shoulders, back and thighs as spots that hold up, and Villani said hands and feet fade quickly.</p>
<p>The size point is the one first-timers fight the hardest. You want it small because small feels safer. But small is where detail goes to die. Let the design dictate the size, not the other way around. If your artist says it needs to be bigger, they've seen what happens when it isn't.</p>
<p>Also think about your next one. A first tattoo dropped dead center on your forearm can make the second piece awkward. Our <a href="/blog/tattoo-placement-guide">tattoo placement guide</a> covers flow and planning for a collection, even if you swear this is the only one. It won't be.</p>
<hr>
<h2>The bottom line</h2>
<p>So, what should you get for your first tattoo? Something you like looking at. It doesn't need a backstory. Classic designs, traditional flash, animals and tributes to the people you love are all strong picks, and bold work will carry them for years.</p>
<p>If it's a portrait, find a specialist and pay for it. If it's a trend or your partner's name, walk away. And if you can't decide, look at the flash and wannados from an artist you already love. The answer is often sitting on their wall.</p>

<h2>FAQ</h2>
<h3>What should I get for my first tattoo?</h3>
<p>Something you like the look of, built bold enough to hold. It doesn't need a deep meaning. Classic designs, traditional flash, animals and tributes to loved ones are all solid first picks. Choose an artist who specializes in that style and let them size it properly.</p>
<h3>Does a first tattoo have to be meaningful?</h3>
<p>No. Plenty of great tattoos exist just because they look good. A panther, rose or swallow you love the look of is a completely legit first tattoo. Meaning can shift over the years, but a well-made design stays a well-made design.</p>

<p>It is a regret removal shops see. A Massachusetts removal shop owner told the Boston Globe that some of his work involves ex-boyfriends' and ex-girlfriends' names. Get a tattoo about you instead.</p>
<h3>How much does a portrait tattoo cost?</h3>
<p>It depends on the artist, size and detail. One studio blog, Certified Tattoo Studios, puts portrait work at roughly $150 to $250 an hour and says most portraits need at least two sessions. Treat that as a ballpark and go to a reputable portrait specialist.</p>

<p>A wannado is a design or idea an artist personally wants to tattoo, often posted for clients to claim. One artist's booking form describes them as ideas the artist wants to do more of or experiment with. Picking one usually gets you the artist at their most motivated.</p>

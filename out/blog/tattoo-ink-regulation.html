Tattoo Ink Regulation: What the FDA and State Rules Actually Require

Tattoo ink regulation explained: what FDA's 2024 guidance covers, who it applies to, how state rules differ, and what to ask your artist before you sit down.

Mark Dupuis·October 5, 2026

TL;DR: FDA's October 2024 tattoo ink guidance is a set of recommendations for ink manufacturers and distributors. It isn't a law, and it puts no new duties on you or your artist. Tattoo ink counts as a cosmetic, FDA has not approved any pigment for injection, and states and local governments regulate the shop itself. Separate cosmetics law makes ink companies report serious adverse events. Here's what's confirmed and what isn't.

Tattoo ink regulation is one of those topics where a headline says "new rules" and nobody can tell you what actually changed. Is your artist suddenly on the hook? Is your favorite black getting pulled? Not based on anything we found.

Here's the real starting point. In a study published in July 2024, FDA researchers tested 75 sealed tattoo and permanent makeup inks from 14 manufacturers and found bacteria in 26 of them. A few months later, FDA put out final guidance aimed at the companies that make and sell the ink. That guidance, plus a 2022 law that updated how FDA handles cosmetics, is most of what's "new."

This post sticks to what we could confirm on official sources: FDA pages, the Federal Register, state legislature and health department sites, and EU law. Where something is unclear, we say so. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is Tattoo Ink Actually Regulated in the US?

Yes, as a cosmetic. FDA treats tattoo ink as a cosmetic and oversees the ink itself. It doesn't pre-approve cosmetics, with the exception of some color additives, and FDA says none of those are approved for injection into the skin. The practice of tattooing is regulated by state and local governments.

That "none approved" line sounds scarier than it is, so read it carefully. FDA's tattoo page says that although a number of color additives are approved for use in cosmetics, none are approved for injection into the skin. It doesn't say every ink on the market is illegal. It says the approval system for cosmetic color doesn't cover tattooing.

In practice, FDA's lever has been contamination. Under the federal cosmetics law, a cosmetic prepared, packed or held under insanitary conditions counts as adulterated, and FDA's guidance applies that to tattoo ink. That's the thread that runs through everything below.

What Did FDA's October 2024 Tattoo Ink Guidance Actually Say?

It recommends that ink manufacturers and distributors test inks and ingredients for microbes, make sure their manufacturing process doesn't add contamination, validate any sterilization they claim, and keep contaminated product from shipping. FDA calls it current thinking, not a binding rule. It isn't addressed to tattoo artists.

The final guidance says it "does not establish any rights for any person and is not binding on FDA or the public." It was announced in the Federal Register on October 25, 2024 and is written for "tattoo ink manufacturers and distributors." A footnote adds that FDA does not regulate practices in tattoo parlors, though much of the guidance may be helpful to them.

It's also pretty specific about what goes wrong at the factory level. Examples include mixing or packing ink in spaces that are hard to clean (carpeted areas), leaving ink and ingredients uncovered near air ducts, using containers that haven't been cleaned and sanitized, and staff skipping hairnets, gowns or gloves. None of that is about your artist's station. It's about how the bottle got made.

Why Is FDA Focused on Contamination?

Because ink goes into the dermis, and microbes that are harmless on top of the skin can cause problems underneath. FDA's guidance says contaminated ink has caused infections, including a 2012 multi-state outbreak of nontuberculous mycobacterial skin infections, and that testing has found many sealed inks contaminated.

The numbers FDA points to are worth knowing. FDA's final guidance announcement says firms conducted 18 voluntary recalls of contaminated tattoo inks between 2003 and 2024, the most recent on August 23, 2024. The guidance cites a 2018 survey in the Journal of Applied Microbiology that found 49 percent of surveyed inks on the U.S. market were contaminated.

Then there's the 2024 FDA study in Applied and Environmental Microbiology: 26 of 75 ink samples were contaminated. The authors found no significant association between a "sterile" claim on the label and a lack of contamination. That's the part that should change how you think about a sticker on a bottle.

One honest caveat. Those numbers describe the inks that were tested. They don't tell you the odds that your tattoo gets infected, and we didn't find an official figure for that. Contamination is also a separate question from what ink does in the body long term, which we covered in tattoos and lymphoma risk.

Does MoCRA Change Anything for Tattoo Ink?

Somewhat, for the companies that make it. MoCRA, the 2022 law that updated FDA's cosmetics authority, requires the company named on a cosmetic's label to report serious adverse events to FDA within 15 business days, and it gave FDA mandatory recall authority. Those reporting duties sit with the company, not with you or your artist.

Here's how FDA describes the reporting rule: a "responsible person," meaning the manufacturer, packer or distributor whose name is on the label, must report serious adverse events within 15 business days. The definition of serious includes infection and significant disfigurement. FDA's own tattoo ink guidance flags the new reporting requirement in a footnote. FDA's cosmetics law page also lists mandatory recall authority as one of the new powers.

MoCRA also requires cosmetic facilities to register and products to be listed. FDA delayed enforcement of those requirements until July 1, 2024, and in a May 2026 post it reported over 15,000 active facility registrations across all cosmetics.

Two things we could not pin down. FDA says small-business exemptions from registration, listing and good manufacturing practice rules don't apply to products that are injected or that alter appearance for more than 24 hours, but its page never names tattoo ink, so we won't guess how that plays out for any one brand. And the guidance says FDA intends to write cosmetic manufacturing rules under MoCRA. We couldn't confirm a final version exists, so treat that as pending.

Which Claims Floating Around Online Didn't Check Out?

A few specifics keep showing up in posts about new tattoo ink laws. We checked them against official sources, and here's where they landed.

  • "Mandatory facility registration by 2026." FDA's own page puts the start of enforcement at July 1, 2024, and registration is already live. That date has passed.
  • "Oregon HB 3042." We looked up HB 3042 in Oregon's legislative system for every regular session from 2017 to 2025. They cover culturally specific health care, aerial chemical application, a state government study, publicly supported housing, and the Board of Naturopathic Medicine. The 2025 version is about naturopathic medicine. None mention tattoos or ink.
  • "15 business days for adverse event reports." Real, but it applies to the responsible person on the label. Artists and clients aren't the ones with that deadline.

If a source gives you a bill number or a deadline for tattoo ink, look it up on the legislature's or FDA's own site before you repeat it.

How Do State Rules Differ From Federal Ones?

There's no single national rulebook for tattoo shops. FDA says state and local jurisdictions generally regulate the practice of tattooing, so what applies to your artist depends on where the shop is. Oregon and New York show how different those rules can look.

Oregon regulates this through its Health Licensing Office. Under OAR 331-915-0065, inks, dyes or pigments must be bought from a commercial supplier or manufacturer and have an expiration date on the container, and using expired ink is prohibited. The rule also bars products banned or restricted by FDA. The state's tattoo rules add that licensees must dilute inks with sterile or distilled water and store them so touch and air particles can't contaminate them. Those rules apply to license holders and licensed facilities.

New York works differently. Public Health Law section 461 requires a permit to be a tattooist or run a tattoo studio. Section 467 says tattoo studios "shall use only single use needles and approved ink handling procedures," and requires a confirmation form signed by the client and the tattooist, which the studio keeps for at least seven years. "Approved ink handling procedures" means procedures the state health department has approved.

Notice what both states regulate: the shop and the artist. In the sections we read, neither is about how an ink maker runs its factory. That's the part FDA's guidance is aimed at.

What Does Europe Do Differently?

Europe went at ingredients rather than contamination. Commission Regulation (EU) 2020/2081 added a restriction on substances in tattoo inks and permanent makeup, applying from 4 January 2022. It covers substances classified as carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic to reproduction, skin sensitizers, skin corrosives and irritants, and more.

It also sets labelling rules. Suppliers have to mark mixtures as "Mixture for use in tattoos or permanent make-up," add a unique batch reference and a full ingredient list, and include statements about nickel and chromium (VI). Pigment Blue 15:3 and Pigment Green 7 got an extra year, until 4 January 2023.

That's a different tool for a different problem. The EU rule is about what's in the ink. FDA's 2024 guidance is about whether the ink is clean.

What Should You Do Before Your Next Tattoo?

Ask your artist what ink they use and where it comes from, check that the container has an expiration date, and don't treat a "sterile" label as a guarantee. FDA's own advice is that there's no surefire way to tell if ink is safe without testing, even if the container is sealed.

None of that is a legal requirement for you, and a good artist won't be offended. If you're newer to this, our guide to how to vet a tattoo artist covers portfolios, healed work and shop setup, and the first tattoo guide walks through what to expect in the chair.

Afterward, watch the area. FDA's consumer advice says that if the area seems not to be healing, or you notice a rash, redness or bumps near the tattoo, contact your tattooist and your health care professional, especially if you develop a fever. FDA also notes that infections can be hard to recognize because other conditions, like allergic reactions, can look similar at first. That's a good reason to get it looked at rather than guess. Our tattoo aftercare guide and tattoo healing stages cover what normal looks like.

If you do have a bad reaction, tell FDA. Its cosmetic complaint page lists the MedWatch voluntary reporting form, paper forms 3500 (health professionals) and 3500B (consumers), and the Food and Cosmetics Information Center at 1-888-SAFEFOOD, option 4. Include the ink brand and what happened.

Here's the short version. The headline "new tattoo ink rules" overstates it. What's real: FDA's 2024 guidance for ink makers, a 2022 cosmetics law with adverse event reporting and recall powers, and state rules that vary by where you get tattooed. What's not real, or not confirmed: the deadlines and bill numbers floating around online that we couldn't find on an official source.

FAQ

Does the FDA approve tattoo ink? No. FDA does not pre-approve cosmetics, with the exception of some color additives, and it says none of those are approved for injection into the skin. It does oversee tattoo ink as a cosmetic and can act on contaminated products.

Is FDA's tattoo ink guidance a law? No. The October 2024 guidance is written for ink manufacturers and distributors, and FDA says it is not binding. It recommends testing for microbes, preventing contamination during manufacturing, validating sterilization and stopping contaminated product from shipping.

Who regulates tattoo shops, FDA or the state? State and local governments generally regulate the practice of tattooing, including shop conditions and licensing. FDA regulates the ink as a cosmetic but says it does not regulate practices in tattoo parlors. Rules differ by state, so check your own state or county health department.

Is "sterile" tattoo ink actually sterile? Not necessarily. A 2024 FDA study of 75 inks found 26 contaminated and no significant association between sterility claims on the label and lack of contamination. FDA's advice is that there's no surefire way to tell if ink is safe without testing.

How do I report a bad reaction to tattoo ink? You can file a MedWatch voluntary report with FDA online, send paper form 3500B as a consumer, or call 1-888-SAFEFOOD and choose option 4. Give as much detail as you can about the ink and what happened. See a health care professional about any rash, redness, bumps or fever.

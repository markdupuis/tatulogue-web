<p><strong>TL;DR:</strong> Scammers copy a real artist's portfolio, build a fake account, and collect deposits for appointments that don't exist. Platforms are slow to remove them, so the checking falls on you. Before you send a cent, confirm the account through the artist's official website or a phone number for the shop that you found yourself.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<p>You get a DM from a tattoo artist you've followed for a year. They have an opening next week, a discount for followers, and a deposit link to lock it in. You send $100 through a payment app. Then you show up at the shop, and nobody there has ever heard of your booking.</p>
<p>That's the standard shape of tattoo scams right now, and it keeps working because every piece of it looks normal. DMs are how tattoo booking happens. Deposits are how you hold a spot. The portfolio is real, because it was stolen from someone who is.</p>
<p>Here's how the scam works, the red flags that actually matter, and how to check an artist in about five minutes.</p>
<hr>
<h2>How Do Tattoo Scams Actually Work?</h2>
<p>Tattoo scams almost always start with a cloned account. Scammers copy a real artist's photos, videos and portfolio, set up a near-identical profile, and message people who follow the real artist. They collect a deposit through a payment app, then book nothing. The client finds out when they turn up.</p>
<p>It's not a small-time problem. In Jacksonville this summer, <a href="https://www.news4jax.com/news/local/2026/07/22/fake-tiktok-accounts-impersonating-jacksonville-tattoo-artist-scam-customers-out-of-deposits/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">News4JAX reported</a> that a studio owner had at least 10 fake TikTok accounts impersonating her, built from her own videos and portfolio. More than a dozen customers were scammed over six months. Some of the fake profiles even paid for ads.</p>
<p>The studio owner reported the accounts repeatedly and they stayed up. TikTok removed them after the news outlet got in touch.</p>
<p>That pattern isn't new. Back in 2023, a Salt Lake City shop told <a href="https://www.ksl.com/article/50696654/salt-lake-city-tattoo-shop-warns-of-fake-social-media-profiles-scams" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">KSL</a> that at least 50 people had reported fake accounts using its artists' names. Same story: the deposits vanished, and Facebook and Instagram declined to take the accounts down.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Why Are Tattoo Clients Such an Easy Target?</h2>
<p>Because the normal booking process looks exactly like the scam. Real artists really do take deposits, really do book over DM, and really do sometimes take them through payment apps. There's no obvious line between "legit" and "fake" until you check.</p>
<p>Scammers also pick the moments when attention is high. A fresh flash sale, a giveaway, a guest spot announcement. Followers are primed to jump on an opening, and they message fast.</p>
<p>Scale helps them too. Social media is where a lot of fraud now starts. The FTC says nearly 30% of people who reported losing money to a scam in 2025 said it began on social media, with <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2026/04/reported-losses-scams-social-media-eight-times-higher-2020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">$2.1 billion in reported losses</a>, about eight times the 2020 figure. That's all scams, not just tattoo ones. But it tells you where the pressure is.</p>
<p>And the platforms aren't a safety net. The person being impersonated is often the only one Instagram will listen to. <a href="https://help.instagram.com/1291391774792379/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Instagram's own help page</a> says it only responds to impersonation reports from the person being impersonated or a representative. If you spot a fake, tell the real artist.</p>
<hr>
<h2>What Are the Red Flags Before You Send a Deposit?</h2>
<p>The strongest tattoo scam red flags are pressure, an unsolicited DM asking for money, a handle that's slightly off, and an artist you can't find on the shop's own website. Any one of them means stop and verify first.</p>
<p>Shops that deal with this keep coming back to the same list:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>They contacted you first.</strong> <a href="https://www.alchemytattoogallery.com/post/beware-of-tattoo-scammers-how-to-protect-yourself-when-booking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Alchemy Tattoo Gallery</a> flags unsolicited DMs that ask for payment.</li>
<li><strong>The handle is a little wrong.</strong> Extra underscores, a swapped letter, a period where there wasn't one.</li>
<li><strong>There's a rush.</strong> "Only one spot left" or a last-minute follower discount. Logan Square Tattoo <a href="https://www.logansquaretattoo.com/post/tattoo-scammers-and-how-to-avoid-them" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">warns about urgency and surprise deals</a>.</li>
<li><strong>You can't find the artist on the shop's site.</strong> No listing, no booking page, no matching email.</li>
<li><strong>The money goes to a name that doesn't match.</strong> Or to an "assistant."</li>
</ul>
<p>One honest note. Payment apps aren't proof of a scam by themselves. Plenty of real artists take deposits that way. The red flag is sending money before you've confirmed who you're paying.</p>
<hr>
<h2>How Do You Verify an Artist Is Real?</h2>
<p>Go around the DM. Find the shop's website yourself, check that the artist is listed there, and contact the shop through the phone number or email on that site. Confirm the handle matches across platforms, then confirm the deposit details with the shop, not the DM.</p>
<p>Here's the five-minute version:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Search the shop, not the artist.</strong> Find the studio's own site and see whether this artist is listed.</li>
<li><strong>Compare handles across platforms.</strong> A real artist is usually consistent on Instagram, TikTok and their site. Mismatches are a bad sign.</li>
<li><strong>Check the availability.</strong> Surprise openings are rare, and real ones usually show up in the artist's own posts or stories, as Alchemy points out.</li>
<li><strong>Call or email the shop.</strong> Use the contact info from the shop's website, not the one in the DM.</li>
<li><strong>Ask where the deposit goes.</strong> If it's a booking platform or the shop's own payment page, good. If it's a personal account with a name that doesn't match, stop.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you're new to booking, our guide to <a href="/blog/how-to-vet-a-tattoo-artist">how to vet a tattoo artist</a> goes deeper on portfolios, healed work and consultations. And <a href="/blog/instagram-failing-tattoo-artists">how Instagram fails tattoo artists</a> explains why the platform makes this problem worse.</p>
<hr>
<h2>What If You Already Sent Money?</h2>
<p>Act fast, and be realistic. Contact the payment app right away, report the account to the platform, and file a report with the FTC. Recovery isn't guaranteed. In the Salt Lake City case some victims did get money back through PayPal and Venmo disputes, and the scammers' payment accounts were shut down.</p>
<p>Here's the order to work through:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Contact your payment app.</strong> Do it right away and tell them it was a scam. Payment apps generally treat a payment you sent yourself as authorized, so recovery isn't a given, but you lose nothing by asking. Logan Square recommends using any buyer protection the app offers.</li>
<li><strong>Report the fake account.</strong> Tell the platform, and tell the real artist so they can file their own impersonation report.</li>
<li><strong>Report it to the FTC</strong> at <a href="https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ReportFraud.ftc.gov</a>. It can't resolve your individual case, but reports are shared with law enforcers and used to build cases.</li>
<li><strong>Keep everything.</strong> Screenshots of the DMs, the account, the payment.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you're going to pay a deposit on a payment app at all, use the option that carries buyer protection when there is one, or a card. It's not foolproof, but it's better than sending cash-equivalent money to a stranger.</p>

<h2>FAQ</h2>
<p><strong>How do I know if a tattoo artist's Instagram is fake?</strong>
Look for a handle that's slightly different from the one on the shop's website, an unsolicited DM asking for a deposit, and pressure to book fast. The clearest test is to contact the shop through its own website and ask whether the artist works there.</p>
<p><strong>Is it safe to pay a tattoo deposit through Cash App or Venmo?</strong>
Many real artists take deposits this way, so the app alone isn't a red flag. The risk is paying before you've verified who you're paying. Confirm the artist with the shop first, and use an option with buyer protection or a card when you can.</p>
<p><strong>Can I get my tattoo deposit back from a scammer?</strong>
Sometimes. In a 2023 Salt Lake City case, some victims recovered money through PayPal and Venmo disputes. Contact the payment app immediately, but don't count on a refund.</p>
<p><strong>Why don't Instagram and TikTok remove these accounts?</strong>
Sometimes they do, but slowly. Instagram says it only acts on impersonation reports from the person being impersonated or a representative, and in the Jacksonville case TikTok removed the accounts only after a news outlet contacted it. If you spot a fake, tell the real artist.</p>
<p><strong>How do I report a fake tattoo artist account?</strong>
Tell the real artist so they can file an impersonation report with the platform. Report the scam to your payment app, and file a report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.</p>

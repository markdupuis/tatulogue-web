Are Tattoos Linked to Lymphoma? What the Actual Research Says

A 2024 Swedish study raised the question of tattoos and lymphoma risk. Here's what the study actually found, why researchers pushed back on the framing, and what's still unclear.

Tatulogue Team·September 21, 2026

TL;DR: A 2024 Swedish study found a modest, statistically weak association between tattoos and lymphoma, and the epidemiologist who reviewed it called the conclusion "really overstated." Newer research on tattoos and skin cancer is genuinely mixed, not alarming. Even for an individual person, the added risk this study suggests works out to a small number either way. Here's what's actually confirmed versus still unclear.

A headline made the rounds a couple years back: tattoos linked to lymphoma. It got shared fast, stripped of context, and landed in a lot of group chats sounding scarier than the research behind it actually supports. If you've got ink, or you're planning your next piece, and that headline's been sitting in the back of your mind, here's the real breakdown of tattoos and lymphoma risk: what the study found, what outside experts said about it, and what's still genuinely unsettled.

This isn't a piece telling you to panic, and it's not a piece telling you there's nothing to it either. It's what the research actually shows, without the clickbait framing.

What Did the Original Tattoo-Lymphoma Study Actually Find?

A 2024 study out of Lund University in Sweden looked at 11,905 people in Sweden's national registers and found tattoos were somewhat more common among people diagnosed with malignant lymphoma than among matched controls: 21% of lymphoma cases had tattoos versus 18% of controls, which worked out to roughly a 21% higher relative rate, concentrated in B-cell lymphoma subtypes.

The study, published in eClinicalMedicine and announced by Lund University, was a population-based case-control study built from Sweden's National Cancer Register and Total Population Register. That's an observational design, which can surface a statistical association between two things without proving one causes the other. The researchers themselves flagged real limits: a low survey response rate of 47% to 54%, and notably, no clear dose-response relationship. More tattoos or bigger tattoos didn't track with higher lymphoma rates in any consistent pattern, which is exactly what you'd expect to see if ink exposure were actually driving the risk.

Why an Epidemiologist Called the Framing Overstated

The pushback came fast, and it came from someone with real standing on the topic. Dr. Timothy Rebbeck, an epidemiologist at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute and a professor at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, reviewed the study for CNN's health coverage and didn't hold back: "If I were writing that paper, if I were the editor, I would have said the conclusion is, there is no evidence for a strong association."

Rebbeck's core objection was statistical. The much-quoted 21% added risk, he said, "is not statistically significant." He added that a smaller 2023 study looking at the same question also found no increased risk, and summed up his read of the whole thing bluntly: "We really didn't learn a lot about whether tattoos are associated with cancer with this."

A separate write-up in the Journal of the French Society of Dermatology, indexed on ScienceDirect, reached a similarly skeptical conclusion in its own title: that the recent tattoo-lymphoma study "does not support a possible association" strong enough to justify the alarm the original headlines generated. The state of the science, as of this research, is that something worth studying further showed up in one dataset, not that tattoos have been shown to cause cancer.

What's Actually Known About Tattoos and Skin Cancer

If you go looking for a follow-up answer on tattoos and cancer risk more broadly, the honest answer is that it's a mess of conflicting results, not a confirmed trend in either direction. A 2025 Utah-based case-control study, published in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute, found something genuinely strange: people with a single tattoo session had a higher rate of melanoma, but people with heavier tattoo exposure, four or more sessions, or three or more large tattoos, showed a notably lower rate. The study's own authors pointed to likely confounding, meaning people who get extensively tattooed may differ systematically in sun exposure, tanning bed use, or other melanoma risk factors that the study didn't measure, rather than tattoos themselves doing anything protective or harmful.

Other research points in different directions entirely. A large French study of around 110,000 people found no association at all between having tattoos and developing skin cancer. Put those results next to each other and you get exactly what real, unsettled science looks like: multiple credible studies, no consistent pattern.

UPMC HealthBeat's 2026 rundown of this research landscape lands on the same conclusion: the evidence is "ongoing and currently unclear," and no study has established a proven causal link between tattoos and any cancer type. Their practical advice is the same advice dermatologists give everyone regardless of ink: wear sunscreen, avoid unnecessary sun exposure, and get regular skin checks.

How Tattoo Ink Actually Moves Through Your Body

Here's the part that explains why researchers are looking at lymph nodes in the first place, and it's not controversial. Harvard Health's breakdown of the research notes that pigment from tattoo ink can show up in enlarged lymph nodes within weeks of getting tattooed. Immune cells in the skin react to the chemicals in tattoo ink and carry some of it toward nearby lymph nodes as part of a completely ordinary immune response.

That's a known biological fact, not evidence of harm on its own. The open question is whether that ink migration, over time and volume, does anything meaningful to lymph node tissue, or whether it's just biologically inert once it settles there. Nobody has a confirmed answer yet either way.

Harvard Health also points out something that cuts against the scarier headlines: nearly all the statistical differences in the Lund data weren't statistically significant, meaning they're "quite possibly observed by chance." And if tattoos really were driving lymphoma risk in a meaningful way, you'd expect lymphoma rates to have climbed alongside tattooing's surge in popularity over the past two decades. That hasn't happened.

So Should You Actually Be Worried?

No, not based on what the research currently shows. Lymphoma is one of the more common cancer types overall, but a modest, statistically shaky association from a single observational study isn't a reason to cancel a booking or regret existing work. The epidemiologist most directly critical of the study's framing said it himself: there isn't strong evidence here.

What it is, is a reason to pay attention as more research comes out. Science works by generating a signal, then testing it harder to see if it holds up under scrutiny. Right now, that process is producing a genuinely mixed picture, not a confirmed danger, and outlets covering it responsibly are landing on "unclear" rather than "alarming."

If anything changes that meaningfully, we'll cover it. For now, the honest summary is: one dataset showed a weak signal, the epidemiologist who reviewed it called the framing overstated, and the broader research on tattoos and cancer risk is inconsistent enough that no one, including the researchers involved, is calling this settled.

FAQ

Do tattoos cause lymphoma? No study has shown that tattoos cause lymphoma. A 2024 Swedish study found a modest statistical association, but the epidemiologist who reviewed the findings said the added risk wasn't statistically significant and the conclusion was overstated.

How strong was the link found in the 2024 tattoo-lymphoma study? The study found tattoos were somewhat more common among people with lymphoma, about 21% versus 18% in matched controls, with no clear pattern showing more tattoos or larger tattoos increasing that risk further. Outside reviewers called the association statistically weak.

Does tattoo size affect skin cancer risk? The research is genuinely mixed. One 2025 study found heavier tattoo exposure linked to lower melanoma rates while light exposure linked to higher rates, likely due to confounding factors the study couldn't measure. A separate large study found no association at all.

Why does tattoo ink end up in lymph nodes? Immune cells react to the chemicals in tattoo ink and carry some of it toward nearby lymph nodes, a normal, well-documented immune response, not itself evidence of harm.

Should I avoid getting tattooed because of this research? Based on current evidence, no. The association found so far is statistically weak, the researcher most critical of the study's framing said there's no strong evidence, and the broader cancer research is too inconsistent to point to a real risk either way.

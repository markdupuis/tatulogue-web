<p><strong>TL;DR:</strong> You want a tattoo but you don't know what to call the thing you want — which means you can't find the right artist for it. This is the practical breakdown: what each style actually is, how it ages, and who it's not for. Use it to book smarter.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>You walk into a shop and point at something on the wall. The artist asks what style you're going for. You shrug. That's not a great start.</p>
<p>Style isn't just aesthetics. It's technique, aging, artist specialisation, and what you'll be living with for the next thirty years. Getting the style wrong means booking the wrong artist. Getting the wrong artist means a tattoo that doesn't hold.</p>
<p>This is a buyer's guide for your skin.</p>
<h2>American Traditional</h2>
<p>Bold black outlines, limited colour palette, flat fills. Eagles, panthers, roses, anchors, daggers. The visual language of the style has been consistent for over a hundred years because it works.</p>
<p>Traditional ages better than almost anything else. The bold outlines hold the design together even as the colour inside them softens over time. A clean trad piece will outlive trends every time. Packed black and bold linework still age better than half the trendy Pinterest stuff floating around right now.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> Anyone who wants hyper-detailed realism or delicate single-needle linework. Traditional isn't a compromise — it's a commitment to the aesthetic.</p>
<h2>Japanese / Irezumi</h2>
<p>Large-scale compositions built around koi, dragons, peonies, waves, tigers. Designed to flow with the body — the piece is mapped to the muscle and form, not just placed on it. <a href="/blog/japanese-tattoo-history-iconography">Japanese tattooing has centuries of cultural history</a> behind its visual language, and the iconography has specific meaning.</p>
<p>Requires a specialist. The scale, body mapping, and technical requirements mean you can't just find a generalist and hand them a koi reference. Look for artists who only do Japanese work, or who do it as their primary focus. Check their large-scale pieces, not just the smaller flash-style work.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who want something small and quick. A proper Japanese piece is a commitment — often a sleeve, a back piece, or at minimum a large panel. Fragments of the style done small rarely look right.</p>
<h2>Blackwork</h2>
<p>Solid black, heavy fills, graphic forms. Can range from geometric abstraction to dark illustrative work to large blackout panels. The boldest end of the spectrum.</p>
<p>Packed black holds the longest of any technique. If you want something that still looks intentional in twenty years without maintenance, blackwork delivers. The <a href="/blog/tattoo-trends-2026">growth in blackwork</a> has been consistent for a decade because collectors who've seen their other work age are choosing it deliberately.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who might want the piece removed later — blackwork is the hardest style to remove via laser. Also not ideal for collectors who want colour, detail, or softness in their work.</p>
<h2>Fine Line / Single Needle</h2>
<p>Delicate linework, minimal fills, the drawing-like quality that's dominated feeds for the last five years. Looks incredible fresh. Ages more variably than most styles.</p>
<p>The issue isn't the style — it's the execution and placement. <a href="https://tatulogue.com/blog/fine-line-tattoos-complete-guide">Fine line on the wrong placement or from the wrong artist</a> turns soft and blurred inside a few years. Done right, on a stable placement, by a specialist with healed portfolio work to show — it can hold clean for a decade.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who want something that requires zero maintenance or touch-ups. Fine line is higher maintenance than bold work. Also not great on high-movement placements like fingers and inner wrist crease.</p>
<h2>Black and Grey Realism</h2>
<p>Photorealistic rendering in black and grey. Portraits, nature studies, detailed animal work. The technical ceiling for this style is extremely high.</p>
<p>Good black and grey realism looks stunning and, done with the right technique, holds well. The key word is right technique — <a href="/blog/overworked-skin-tattoo">artists who over-work the skin</a> trying to achieve photorealism can cause scarring and poor ink retention. This style rewards doing real research on the artist.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People on a budget looking for the cheapest version of this style. Realism done cheap almost always becomes mush. Save and book the artist who can actually do it.</p>
<h2>Neo Traditional</h2>
<p>Takes the bold outlines of American traditional and the limited palette, but pushes the illustration into more contemporary subjects and more elaborate linework. Animals, portraits, botanicals — all with more dynamism and detail than classic trad allows.</p>
<p>Ages similarly to traditional — the bold outlines provide structure that holds the piece together over time. More flexibility on subject matter than classic trad without sacrificing longevity.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who think it's the same as American traditional. The technique overlaps but the aesthetic is different. Bring the right reference and make sure your artist specialises in the neo-trad style specifically.</p>
<h2>Geometric</h2>
<p>Precise, mathematically exact forms. Mandalas, sacred geometry, structured linework that depends on total accuracy. The margin for error is smaller here than in almost any other style.</p>
<p>Geometric tattoos look incredible when executed precisely. A single misaligned line or uneven spacing is immediately visible. You need an artist who is technically exact — this isn't a style where "good enough" works.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> Anyone who thinks they can get this style at a discount. Precision costs. Also not ideal on curved or difficult-to-access placements where maintaining a straight line is harder for the artist.</p>
<h2>Watercolour</h2>
<p>Mimics the look of watercolour paint — loose washes of colour, soft edges, no traditional outlines. The style that gets the most questions about aging, and for good reason.</p>
<p><a href="/blog/watercolour-tattoos-how-they-age">Watercolour tattoos fade faster than virtually any other style</a>. Without bold outlines to hold the design, the colour washes bleed and soften significantly over time. The look that attracted you to the style is often the first thing to go. Most artists who work in watercolour will tell you this honestly — if yours doesn't, that's information.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who want something that looks the same in ten years as it did fresh. Watercolour is a style that requires expectation-setting going in.</p>
<h2>Tribal / Polynesian</h2>
<p>Geometric black patterns rooted in Samoan, Maori, Filipino, and other Pacific Islander traditions. The designs have cultural and ancestral meaning in their original context.</p>
<p>These are some of the most durable tattoos on the body — solid black, bold forms, ages extremely well. If you're approaching the style from a place of genuine respect for the tradition, find an artist from that culture or who has deep roots in it. There are brilliant Polynesian tattoo artists doing this work correctly.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who want a vague "tribal look" disconnected from the cultural context. Do the research, find the right artist, approach it properly.</p>
<h2>New School</h2>
<p>Exaggerated, cartoon-influenced, heavy outlines and saturated colour. Bigger than traditional, louder, more stylised. A style that rewards artists with a strong illustration background.</p>
<p>Ages well when done with the bold linework the style calls for. The heavy outlines do the same work they do in traditional — hold the design together as the fill colour softens. A well-executed new school piece holds its readability long-term.</p>
<p><strong>Who this is NOT for:</strong> People who want something subtle. New school is loud by design. If you're on the fence about how much visual impact you want, this probably isn't the call.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Finding the Right Artist</h2>
<p>Knowing the style is step one. Finding someone who specialises in it — not just someone who will attempt it — is step two.</p>
<p>Portfolio depth matters. An artist who does nothing but blackwork and has fifty blackwork pieces to show is a better bet than someone who'll do any style and has three blackwork pieces in their portfolio. Volume means reps. Reps mean consistency.</p>
<p>Healed work matters more than fresh work. Fresh photos of any style look better than the healed reality. Ask for work that's at least two years old. If an artist's only portfolio is from the last six months, they don't have a healed track record yet.</p>

<h2>FAQ</h2>
<p><strong>Which tattoo style ages best?</strong>
American traditional ages the most reliably — bold outlines and packed fills hold their structure over decades. Blackwork and Japanese traditional also age extremely well. Fine line and watercolour are the most variable, and depend heavily on placement, artist skill, and maintenance.</p>
<p><strong>What tattoo style is best for a first tattoo?</strong>
Traditional or neo-traditional for longevity and legibility. The bold outlines mean the piece will still read clearly as it ages. Fine line can work for a first tattoo but requires more care in artist selection and placement. Avoid highly detailed realism for a first piece — it's harder to execute and harder to cover or adjust later.</p>
<p><strong>What's the difference between black and grey and blackwork?</strong>
Blackwork uses solid black — bold fills, graphic forms, no grey. Black and grey realism uses diluted black ink to create shading, gradients, and photorealistic rendering. They're completely different techniques and require different specialists.</p>
<p><strong>Can I mix tattoo styles in a sleeve?</strong>
With a skilled artist, yes. Patchwork sleeves with contrasting styles are common and can look intentional. The challenge is cohesion — the artist needs to understand how to make the pieces relate to each other visually. It's harder to pull off than a single-style sleeve but completely possible with the right person.</p>
<p><strong>How do I find a tattoo artist who specialises in a specific style?</strong>
Ask for style-specific portfolio work, not their general portfolio. Look at healed examples specifically. Search by style on platforms like <a href="https://app.tatulogue.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Tatulogue</a> to filter for artists who focus on what you're looking for. Don't book a generalist for a style that requires a specialist.</p>
